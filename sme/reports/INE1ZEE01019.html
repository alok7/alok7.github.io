1. Executive Verdict

Shanti Inorganics makes sulphur-based inorganic chemicals for food, industrial, water-treatment and oilfield applications. Its established customer access, profitable manufacturing and partly financed expansion are real strengths. However, original CARO reports repeat unreconciled bank-inventory statements across FY2024–26; the financial impact remains unquantified. Headline FY2026 sales growth of 24.72% also overstates manufacturing growth of 12.20%, because low-spread trading supplies roughly half the increase. At ₹205, about 34.6× restated FY2026 profit on the actual post-IPO share count, neither clean capital-return evidence nor a supported funding-linked base return is available. Classification: Hold — unresolved financial reliability and capital/funding evidence. BQS, Growth Quality and IAS are NS, not zero, midpoint estimates or investable rankings. This is a capacity-led chemical growth transition, not an evidence-backed compounder. A reconciled audit/control record, usable post-IPO cash/debt/object schedule and demonstrated plant economics could change the judgment. FP AR24 AUD25 AR26

4. Business Overview

The portfolio includes ammonium bisulphite, sodium bisulphite solution/powder, sodium metabisulphite and sodium sulphite anhydrous. These are preservatives, reducing agents, oxygen scavengers and process intermediates. Revenue arises from manufactured chemicals and, newly material in FY2026, chemical trading. Costs include liquid sulphur dioxide, alkali/ammonia, conversion energy, packaging, freight, labour and customer credit. Sale recognition is accrual-based; reported profit is not a cash collection. Cash is tied to receivables, stock, export/GST balances and the timing of supplier payments. FP pp173–194,242–248

Vatva Unit I produces several interchangeable bisulphite/sulphite grades within its 18,800-tonne capacity. Bavla Phase I is a distinct 18,000-tonne anhydrous line, started in February 2025. These capacities cannot be freely pooled. FY2026 production was 17,409 tonnes at Unit I and 2,702 tonnes at Phase I: 92.60% and 15.01% utilization. Proposed Phase II adds 78,544 tonnes, taking the arithmetic total to 115,344 tonnes, but has not commissioned by the cutoff. Production is not identical to sold tonnage or an audited unit selling price. FP pp104–105,178–189

FY2026 included 64 domestic and 20 overseas customers. Exports were ₹30.0269 crore, down about 1.05%, while domestic sales grew about 53.93%. The major disclosed product-sales end uses were food/beverages 35.84%, chemicals 22.38% and oil drilling 12.82%. These provide varied demand channels but not immunity to cyclicality. Geographic tables sum to revenue from operations; some business-concentration percentages use the smaller net product-sales denominator. FP pp25–30,174,269

5. Moat / Industry Position

The credible mechanism is high-purity processing and quality qualification, combined with Gujarat feedstock/logistics access and repeat customer relationships. Its evidence is actual manufacturing/export revenue, certifications and the CRISIL assessment of purity-related price premium/pass-through. Durability is limited by unquantified switching costs, no registered intellectual property at the prospectus date, contestable chemistry and reliance on purchase orders rather than long-term customer contracts. Failure modes are supplier disruption, grade/approval failure, aggressive rival pricing or a slow new-plant qualification cycle. A certification or customer list is not by itself a moat. FP pp177–189 CR26

The top ten customers supplied 63.35% of FY2026 net product sales; top five suppliers supplied 70.08% of raw-material purchases. In the two months to May, these rose to 71.03% and 84.81%. Short-period concentration is volatile, but the contractual weakness is real. The Gujarat cluster can help procurement while increasing common feedstock exposure.
